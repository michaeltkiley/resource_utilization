Resource Utilization and Risk

How far is output from its potential — four ways of answering the same question.

The CBO gap comes from the Congressional Budget Office's own model of potential GDP. The other three come from two models built by economist Michael Kiley. The structural model gives two: the Beveridge‑Nelson gap, how far output sits from its long‑run trend, and the production‑function gap, how far output sits from what the economy's labor and capital could normally produce. The Unobserved Components (UC) model gives a simpler, complementary estimate — the same model that also produces the equilibrium real interest rate, r*.

BN and PF gaps based on M.T. Kiley (2013), “Output gaps,” Journal of Macroeconomics, 37, 1–18.

UC gap based on M.T. Kiley (2020), “What Can the Data Tell Us about the Equilibrium Real Interest Rate?,” International Journal of Central Banking, 16(3), 181–209.

CBO Output Gap — 100×(real GDP / CBO real potential GDP − 1); CBO's own percent-of-potential convention.
Structural model — the Federal Reserve Board's EDO model (Chung, Kiley & Laforte).
Structural BN Gap — the model's Beveridge‑Nelson decomposition: deviation of output from its long-run stochastic trend.
Structural PF Gap — the model's production-function decomposition: deviation of output from the level consistent with current technology and normal utilization of capital and labor.
Structural-model estimates use the current-analysis treatment: every one of the model's 13 observables is HP-detrended (λ=128000) and re-anchored to the model's own steady-state constants before being Kalman-smoothed, rather than compared to a fixed trend estimated only through 2011:Q4. A paper-exact replication (no detrending) is also maintained; see the output_gap project.
UC Gap — the output gap from a compact Unobserved Components model of GDP growth, unemployment, inflation, long‑run inflation expectations, and the fed funds rate. The same model estimates the equilibrium real interest rate, r*. Estimated with re-optimized (posterior-mode) parameters each update, unlike the structural model's fixed parameters.

How likely — and how large — a sharp rise in unemployment looks from here.

Two models built by economist Michael Kiley estimate the risk of a large increase in the unemployment rate, a particularly close indicator of a U.S. recession. The Full Model combines the unemployment rate, inflation, credit growth, and two financial spreads; the Financial Model uses only the corporate‑bond and yield‑curve spreads, the more conventional recession‑prediction approach. Each is shown as a probability — the chance unemployment rises by an unusually large amount — at both a 1‑year and a 3‑year horizon, plus the implied size of that increase if it happens.

Based on M.T. Kiley (2021), “Unemployment Risk,” Journal of Money, Credit and Banking, 54(5), 1407–1424.

See also “Financial and Macroeconomic Indicators of Recession Risk,” FEDS Notes, Board of Governors of the Federal Reserve System (2022).

Probability — from a logistic regression: the modeled chance that the unemployment rate rises, over the given horizon, by more than its own historical 80th‑percentile move (≈¾ point at 1 year, ≈2 points at 3 years) — roughly the size of move that has always accompanied a recession.
Implied size — from a quantile regression (80th percentile) on the same variables: not whether a large move happens, but how large it would be, in percentage points, at that percentile.
Full Model — unemployment rate, PCE inflation, growth in credit relative to GDP, the Baa corporate‑bond spread, and the 10‑year‑Treasury‑minus‑fed‑funds term spread.
Financial Model — the corporate‑bond spread and term spread only, the conventional financial‑variables approach to recession prediction.
Coefficients are re-estimated each update on an expanding window back to 1965:Q1, with 2020:Q1–2020:Q4 excluded from estimation (both as the starting quarter and whenever the forward‑looking outcome itself falls in that window) so the COVID unemployment spike doesn't distort the fit; a paper-exact replication (fixed 1965:Q1–2019:Q4 sample) is also maintained. The Full Model can lag a quarter or two behind the Financial Model, since it needs BIS credit‑to‑GDP data that publishes with a delay. See the output_gap/unemployment_risk project.
